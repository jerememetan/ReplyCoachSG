3.3 Transformer reply generation (V5)

The RNN experiments suggested that Luong Attention improved reply coherence and relevance, but contextual errors remained. We therefore investigated pretrained Transformer models for V5. Self-attention provides direct connections between tokens and permits greater parallelisation during training than recurrent processing [1]. Our aim was to generate a contextually appropriate reply in Standard English and a natural Singlish counterpart that preserved the same intended meaning. A stronger architecture was treated as a hypothesis to evaluate, rather than a guarantee of contextual understanding.

Development proceeded from mT5 adaptation and style conditioning to English-first rewriting, followed by experiments with larger language models. Throughout these experiments, contextual appropriateness, cross-style meaning preservation and natural Singlish were considered separately. This distinction was necessary because two replies could express the same incorrect answer, while a faithful copy of Standard English could still fail the requested local style.

3.3.1 Initial Training Plan

The main initial model was google/mt5-base, a multilingual encoder–decoder Transformer with approximately 582 million parameters in our implementation. Its multilingual text-to-text pretraining made it a suitable starting point for English-based conversations containing Singlish features [2]. The plan comprised two broad stages: full-model language and conversation adaptation, followed by adaptation to the project reply-generation task. The implementation further divided the first stage into span-corruption adaptation, recorded as Stage 0, and public-dialogue training, recorded as Stage 1. The project-specific phase was recorded as Stage 2 [P1].

During language adaptation, the model continued learning through span corruption on 96,295 text chunks from CoSEM and the NUS SMS Corpus. Fifteen per cent of tokens were masked in spans with a mean length of three tokens. The subsequent public-dialogue phase trained next-reply generation on 76,052 DailyDialog pairs and an 80,000-pair CoSEM sample. DailyDialog examples were assigned the STANDARD prefix and CoSEM examples the LOCAL prefix. These source-based labels supplied broad style supervision; they did not provide paired English and Singlish versions of the same reply.

Project-specific adaptation then used the 190 team-authored training conversations. Each supplied one Standard English target and one local target, giving 380 reply examples. Another 180 reply examples were extracted from the training histories, producing 560 supervised rows. Inputs included explicit Friend/You speaker labels to distinguish the person receiving a message from the person composing the reply. A single Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) adapter learned both styles, with textual task prefixes selecting the requested output. LoRA freezes the adapted base weights and trains small additional matrices [3]. In this configuration, 1,769,472 parameters, approximately 0.30% of the base model size, were trainable.

FP32 was used after FP16 training produced non-finite losses in the project runs. Early comparisons also examined mT5-small, full-model task fine-tuning and LoRA. The settings above describe the historical mT5-base baseline; later experiments changed the learning rate, input budget or adaptation method and should not be treated as repetitions of an identical recipe.

The initial model could produce fluent short replies, but the independently generated English and Singlish versions frequently expressed different decisions or actions. Common problems included changing acceptance into refusal, confusing who should lend or borrow an item, and answering a different question from the one asked. Some local outputs remained Standard English, while others relied on repetitive or unsuitable local markers. An audit of 40 sampled CoSEM reply pairs also found 13 whose replies did not answer their associated messages, identifying noise in the adaptation data [P1]. This was a small data audit, rather than an estimate of error across the entire corpus.

3.3.2 Adaptations

The main change was to generate the reply in English first and then rewrite that exact draft into Singlish. Previously, both outputs were generated independently from the conversation. In the revised pipeline, the LOCAL_REWRITE input contained the conversation and the generated English reply. The same shared mT5 adapter performed both tasks. Training used reviewed English anchors; inference used the model’s own English draft. This gave both outputs a common intended response, while allowing the local version to change grammar and phrasing [P2].

The training targets were also aligned in a separate derivative of the 190-item corpus. Fourteen English targets were corrected, and Gemma4 12B supplied candidate local rewrites. An assistant reviewed the drafts and revised 117 local targets. These were generated and assistant-edited targets, rather than independently validated human labels. The original team-authored records were preserved. Three arms compared the original targets with independent generation, aligned targets with independent generation, and aligned targets with sequential rewriting. All used the same adapted base, rank-16 q/v LoRA, learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁴, batch size 16 and 784 optimizer updates. The source limit increased to 192 tokens, while the output limit remained 48 tokens.

On the same 50 validation conversations, the seed-425 aligned-target independent arm preserved meaning in 19 cases, compared with 17 for the original arm. Adding sequential rewriting increased this to 42 cases. The original and sequential arms were then reviewed across seeds 425, 1 and 2. English-first rewriting produced a repeated improvement in paired meaning and local style, but contextual appropriateness remained approximately unchanged. A correct rewrite could faithfully preserve an incorrect English answer. For example, the model could still ask an uninformed peer for printer instructions or reverse charger ownership. The next experiments therefore investigated the content generator as well as the style rewriter.

3.3.3 Larger Models

We next explored AI Singapore’s SEA-LION-v1-3B, a decoder-only language model pretrained on languages used in Southeast Asia [4]. This provided a larger candidate with regional-language exposure. Early direct English/local training produced useful phrasing on some conversations, but the two styles could still disagree. We therefore applied the English-first pipeline to SEA-LION as well. Recorded examples showed improved preservation of some budget refusals, dietary decisions and return directions, alongside continuing errors in exact times, ownership and completion status [P9].

Public adaptation used 117,634 reply pairs, comprising 53,691 CoSEM and 63,943 DailyDialog pairs. Later training used an audited subset of 768 project conversations and 766 eligible English/local rewrite pairs. The SEA-LION C recipe used rank-8 LoRA, alpha 16 and dropout 0.05, with an effective batch size of eight. English and rewrite peak learning rates were 5 × 10⁻⁵ and 10⁻⁵ respectively. Each stage ran for eight epochs with 5% linear warmup followed by cosine decay. Candidate checkpoints were selected using within-model reference loss, followed by inspection of generated replies [P9].

An initial comparison trained fresh SEA-LION and Qwen adapters for four epochs per task without project public adaptation. A separate retrospective study compared saved outputs on all 50 original validation conversations. The mT5 reference was the historical shared-intent seed-1 checkpoint at update 784; the larger-model references were the audited SEA-LION C pipeline and the full-public-recipe Qwen pipeline. Their exact incoming messages, histories and speaker labels were verified to match. The compared mT5 model used the reviewed 190-conversation task dataset, while the later larger-model pipelines used the audited expanded dataset. This is therefore a practical comparison of complete trained systems, not an isolated test of model size or architecture [P11].

One assistant reviewed each English/local pair using four criteria: English contextual adequacy, local contextual adequacy, meaning preservation, and natural recognizable Singlish. Plausible new personal choices were allowed because the outputs are selectable drafts; explicit contradictions, reversed roles and missed requests were treated as errors. Uncertain judgments were recorded separately and did not count as passes. The review was non-blind, and all inputs had already been used extensively in development. Both replies were judged contextually adequate in 28 mT5 cases, 41 SEA-LION cases and 42 Qwen cases. The larger-model pipelines therefore appeared stronger on contextual response generation in this review. Qwen gave the highest English adequacy count, but its local output more often remained neutral Standard English. SEA-LION achieved the highest combined count, 30/50 compared with 27/50 for Qwen and 23/50 for mT5. The difference between the two larger models was small, and no statistical superiority or generalisation claim is made. The historical three-criterion mT5 study and this four-criterion review used different review definitions and must be interpreted separately.

The case-level results illustrate the trade-off. For ITEM-B-057, the peer asks to borrow the user’s charger. mT5 replied “Sure, I’ll borrow it,” reversing the roles, while SEA-LION and Qwen correctly offered the charger. However, SEA-LION reversed the update direction in ITEM-B-034 by asking the absent peer to explain what they missed; Qwen correctly offered a class summary. Qwen also introduced errors during rewriting: in ITEM-D-038, an English promise to wait for the peer became “wait for me” in the local version. These examples show why fluent English and faithful local rewriting require separate assessment.

The Qwen experiments also prompted a closer examination of whether this dataset strongly tested regional-language understanding. The incoming-message annotations show that 112/190 training messages and 30/50 validation messages list local terms. Singlish is tagged in 113/190 training messages and 31/50 validation messages. Pinyin or Chinese is tagged in only 11/190 training messages and 2/50 validation messages. These are counts of the existing annotations, whose coverage is imperfect, rather than a complete linguistic analysis [P11].

A manual assistant audit of the 50 incoming validation messages classified eight as containing regional lexical content, such as kiasu, dabao, chiong or na li. Fifteen primarily contained regional pragmatic markers, apologies or greetings; eight primarily used colloquial syntax; and nineteen were general English or texting. This classification describes the incoming message only; histories and generated replies can contain additional local features. It separates content vocabulary from particles and grammar for analysis, without implying that the latter require no local competence.

This audit supports a narrower conclusion than claiming regional-language understanding was unimportant. Most validation conversations were short, English-based exchanges with light local features. They placed substantial demands on general conversational skills such as tracking participants, timing and intent, but provided limited coverage of extended regional-language code-switching. Regional interpretation still mattered: Qwen treated na li as the place name “Na Li,” and all three systems failed to answer the underlying meeting-location question in ITEM-E-007. A dedicated regional-language benchmark would be needed to isolate that capability.

Taken together, the evidence supports carrying SEA-LION forward as the main V5 generator candidate because it offered a useful balance of coherent replies and recognizable Singlish. Qwen remained a strong alternative for English content generation, while mT5 provided a smaller reference with improved cross-style consistency after English-first rewriting. This is a provisional candidate choice supported by reused development evidence. The reviewed records do not establish a completed independent final evaluation or a deployment-default change. Final selection should follow a frozen comparison on evaluation material that has not guided further tuning.

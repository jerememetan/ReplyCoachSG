1. Introduction
Overview:
We built Reply Coach to help exchange students compose replies to casual peer messages in
Singapore. Given the recent conversation and the latest incoming message, it suggests a
Standard English reply and a Light Local reply. The user can choose and edit either suggestion
before sending.




Problem Statement:
Singlish is used in everyday online messages in Singapore. Some international students
interviewed by Steele (2008) reported difficulty understanding Singlish when they first arrived in
Singapore. They would need to understand what the sender means in context and decide how
to reply in a tone that feels natural without sounding forced. This is where ReplyCoach comes
in, where it’s able to understand singlish conversations and recommends a local or a standard
message for the user to send. Our goal is to suggest replies that make sense in context and
match the user’s chosen style.

In our proposal, we hypothesized that combining normalized retrieval, a lightweight text
generator, and a contextual reranker will outperform single retrieval or generation baselines in
human evaluation for appropriateness, style accuracy, and general utility.

1.1 Related work and our project approaches

Prior work such as Smart Reply shows how a system can suggest short responses for users to
select in email (Kannan et al., 2016). Reply Coach applies that idea to mixed-language peer
chats in Singapore. We built the application around a team-authored collection of paired
Standard and Light Local replies. We compared word-based retrieval using BM25 (Robertson &
Zaragoza, 2009), semantic retrieval using MiniLM, and reply generation using recurrent models
with attention (Luong et al., 2015) and multilingual transformers. We also tested whether a
contextual reranker could choose better replies. The final system gives users an explicit choice
of style for the same conversation. We compared replies from the different systems to see when
reranking helped and when a single model performed better.

In our initial proposal, we planned to develop Reply Coach in stages, from a basic retrieval
system (V0) to a reranker that could choose among replies from different models (V6). As we
tested each stage, some results led us to revisit the original plan. We changed the transformer
used for reply generation and added a separate step to refine the wording of Light Local replies
(V7). Section 3 explains how each version was built, and Section 4 shows which findings led to
these changes and how they shaped the final demo.

2. Data and preprocessing

2.1 Dataset Generation and Annotation
ReplyCoachSG requires conversational examples containing both Standard English and Light
Local (Singlish) replies. As existing public datasets did not provide the paired responses
required for our specific task, we developed a dataset of 300 human-authored conversations,
with each of the five team members contributing 60 examples.

To ensure diversity in conversational situations, we established nine scenario categories
covering meals and food, study sessions and spaces, course materials, assignments and group
projects, classes and tutorials, meetups and transport, social events and outings, sports and
exercise, and campus services and equipment. Each conversation was also assigned one of
nine incoming-message intents, including invitations, information requests, requests for help,
confirmations, rescheduling, refusals, updates, reminders, and apologies. These categories
helped ensure that the dataset represented different situations and communication purposes
commonly encountered by exchange students in Singapore.

For each example, the author constructed one to three previous conversation turns and an
incoming message, followed by two corresponding replies. The Standard English reply was
written to be clear and easily understood by international students, while the Light Local reply
incorporated natural, restrained Singlish expressions where appropriate. Authors also annotated
the language mix and local terms present in each example.

A common annotation guide was used to maintain consistency across authors. Light Local
replies were required to remain predominantly English, avoid excessive Singlish particles, and
refrain from introducing Mandarin expressions absent from the original conversation. This
ensured that local expressions were used contextually rather than inserted artificially.
   2.2 Data Cleaning and Quality Assurance
   To maintain data quality, we implemented a circular peer-review process in which each member
   independently reviewed another member's 60 examples. Member A reviewed Member B's
   examples, Member B reviewed Member C's, and so on, with Member E reviewing Member A.
   This ensured that every example received one independent review before being accepted.

   Reviewers followed a standardized checklist covering privacy, conversational relevance,
   grammatical correctness, and language-style consistency. Standard English replies had to be
   natural and understandable, while Light Local replies had to use Singlish appropriately without
   excessive slang or unnatural code-switching. Reviewers also checked that replies were
   contextually appropriate and that previous conversation turns provided useful information for
   determining the response. Examples that failed these criteria required correction before
   acceptance.

   Privacy was considered during data generation. Authors were instructed to avoid identifiable
   information, including real names, telephone numbers, student identification numbers, and
   residential details. Additionally, all original examples were human-written, with LLM-generated
   wording prohibited during the authoring process.

   Another challenge was identifying duplicate or highly similar conversations. While exact
   duplicates could be detected through text matching, semantically similar examples were more
   difficult to identify when expressed using different wording. Our data preparation workflow
   therefore incorporated near-duplicate detection and grouping to reduce the risk of closely
   related examples appearing across different dataset partitions.

   2.3 Dataset Splitting

Rather than relying solely on random assignment, the splitting process considered the

fixed random seed of 425 supported reproducibility.

2.4 Preprocessing and Supplementary Data
Each conversation was structured to retain its recent message history, latest incoming
message, speaker information, and paired Standard English and Light Local replies. Preserving
speaker roles and conversational context was important because an appropriate response
depends not only on the latest message but also on who said what previously.

For retrieval, we introduced a lexicon-based normalization and query-expansion step to
improve matching between Standard English, Singlish, and Mandarin expressions. For
example, local terms such as "makan" and "吃饭" were mapped to "eat", while "paiseh"
was mapped to "sorry". Original expressions were preserved alongside their English
equivalents to retain linguistic context. Ambiguous terms were excluded from automatic
normalization to avoid incorrect interpretations.

The 190 reviewed training conversations formed the retrieval bank for BM25 and MiniLM and
provided task-specific supervised examples for reply generation. Given the relatively small size
of our dataset, we also used the external DailyDialog corpus to support general conversational
learning. Additional synthetic examples were subsequently introduced for transformer
adaptation and reranker development. These supplementary datasets served different training
purposes and were kept separate from the original held-out test conversations.

Overall, our data preparation process aimed to maintain consistent annotation quality, preserve
conversational context, and minimize data leakage. However, the limited dataset size and
reliance on five authors constrained the diversity of conversational styles represented. These
limitations motivated the use of supplementary training data and highlight the importance of
expanding the dataset and conducting evaluations with actual exchange students in future work.


3. System development

We developed the system in stages. Retrieval gave us a baseline grounded in human-written
replies. The RNN and transformer models could generate new replies for conversations without
a close match in the reply bank. We then tested whether a common reranker could choose the
strongest reply across these sources. Finally, we tested whether a smaller wording change
could make the Local output fit the requested style better.

In the Streamlit demo, the incoming message and selected recent turns are sent to the
comparison pipeline. BM25, MiniLM, the attention RNN, and SEA-LION provide suggestions for
inspection. The final Standard reply comes directly from SEA-LION. The Light Local path starts
with SEA-LION’s Local reply and applies V7 v2 wording refinement when three distinct variants
are available.

3.1 Retrieval and memory (V0–V2)

We began with retrieval-based methods to establish a baseline for suggesting replies without
generating new text. The retrievers searched our 190 reviewed training conversations, where
each example contained an incoming message, conversation context, and paired Standard
English and Light Local replies. We first implemented TF-IDF and BM25 (V0), which rank stored
examples based on lexical similarity. These methods provided a fast and interpretable starting
point, but they depended heavily on word overlap. Messages expressing similar intentions using
different vocabulary could therefore retrieve different or unsuitable replies.

To address this limitation, we introduced V1, a lexicon-based normalisation step for
selected Singlish and Mandarin expressions. For example, “makan” and “吃饭” were
mapped to the English term “eat”, while “paiseh” was mapped to “sorry”. Rather than
replacing the original message entirely, the system preserved the original tokens and
expanded the retrieval query with their canonical English equivalents. This allowed
retrieval to consider both local expressions and their standard meanings. However, the
lexicon could only handle recognised expressions. Ambiguous words such as “can” and
“one” were deliberately excluded from automatic replacement to avoid introducing
incorrect interpretations.

For V2, we implemented semantic retrieval using the pretrained all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence
encoder. Unlike BM25, which relies on lexical overlap, MiniLM represents messages as dense
numerical embeddings. We encoded stored dialogue examples, including their conversation
context, incoming message, and paired replies, into embeddings. During retrieval, the incoming
message and selected memory were similarly encoded, and cosine similarity was used to rank
the stored examples. The highest-ranked examples supplied existing Standard and Light Local
reply candidates. This allowed the system to identify semantically related messages even when
their wording differed, although semantic similarity alone did not guarantee that the retrieved
reply matched the intended response.

We also investigated conversational memory because the latest incoming message may
depend on information mentioned earlier. We compared three configurations: M0, which uses
no previous messages; M1, which retains the latest three previous messages; and M2, which
uses dynamic memory. M2 always retains the latest two messages and uses BM25 to select up
to two relevant older messages from the previous eight conversation turns. Selected messages
are restored to chronological order before being passed downstream. This design aimed to
preserve important earlier context without including the entire conversation history.

We evaluated BM25 and MiniLM across M0, M1, and M2 using the same 50 development
validation queries, producing 300 configuration-query results. In our local latency
measurements, BM25 averaged approximately 4.70–5.36 milliseconds per query, compared
with 18.57–19.96 milliseconds for MiniLM, excluding retriever initialisation. However, M1 and M2
produced different selected replies for only one of the 50 validation queries. This suggested that
the original validation conversations provided limited opportunities to demonstrate the benefits
of retrieving older context.

We therefore conducted 20 additional long-context diagnostic cases. In one example, a peer
asked, “I'm here already, where are you?” M2 recovered an earlier message specifying the
meeting location near MRT Exit B, which M1 had omitted. However, the retrieved reply still
failed to answer the location question appropriately. This demonstrated that recovering relevant
conversational context and selecting an appropriate response are separate challenges.

The later 60-conversation evaluation reported in Section 4 showed that MiniLM achieved higher
AI-rated appropriateness than BM25 for both reply styles. Standard English appropriateness
increased from 2.787 to 3.037 out of 5, while Light Local appropriateness increased from 3.607
to 3.828. These results suggest that semantic retrieval offered an advantage over lexical
retrieval in this evaluation, although they do not isolate the contribution of memory or establish
that MiniLM consistently performs better in every context.

In the shared comparison pipeline, BM25 and MiniLM each supplied retrieved Standard and
Light Local candidates, together with their source information and retrieval scores. The selected
conversational memory was also passed to downstream processing and candidate ranking.
Retrieval therefore provided grounded, human-written reply alternatives alongside the newly
generated candidates from V4 and V5. Our experiments showed that semantic retrieval and
dynamic memory could address some limitations of lexical matching, but they could not reliably
resolve conversational intent, speaker roles, or earlier commitments on their own. These
limitations motivated the later work on generation and contextual candidate selection.

3.3 Transformer reply generation (V5)
Following the RNN experiments, we explored pretrained Transformers to improve contextual
reply generation (Vaswani et al., 2017). The goal was to produce a relevant Standard English
reply and a natural Singlish counterpart with the same meaning. We began with mT5-base, an
approximately 582-million-parameter multilingual encoder–decoder model (Xue et al., 2021).
3.3.1 Initial Training Plan
Training had two main stages. First, full-model adaptation used informal text and public reply
pairs. Reconstructing masked text spans exposed mT5 to local language, while next-reply
training taught conversational response generation. Second, one shared LoRA adapter learned
from 190 project conversations, with STANDARD and LOCAL input prefixes selecting the reply
style (Hu et al., 2021).
This transfer-learning strategy used broader data before specialising on the small project set:
CoSEM supplied Singapore English messages, NUS SMS supplied informal text, and
DailyDialog supplied English dialogues. Without intermediate adaptation, more task and style
learning would depend on the 190 conversations, with a greater risk of memorisation. This was
the rationale; we did not measure the isolated effect of removing this stage.

Decoding strategy
We adopted four-beam search with repeated three-grams blocked because it produced cleaner,
more complete replies than greedy decoding in reviewed examples. The mT5 pipeline allowed
48 generated tokens and the later SEA-LION/Qwen pipelines 64. These decoding choices did
not resolve the remaining role and timing errors.

3.3.4 Distillation
We tested whether the larger teachers could improve mT5 A: the existing model with a
backbone already adapted on local text and public dialogue, plus its trained shared task
adapter. All four training arms continued that same checkpoint. Earlier fresh-adapter
experiments are outside this comparison.
From the 768 training conversations, we retained the common 571 cases with eligible original
pairs and accepted English/Singlish outputs from both teachers after assistant review. For each
teacher, one arm learned its reply text through cross-entropy; another added soft knowledge
distillation (KD), using teacher probability information as well as text. Different vocabularies
required learned alignment modules, so KD changed both the objective and training
components.
Each arm completed 12 epochs, continuing a rank-16 query/value LoRA adapter for both tasks
while freezing the 582-million-parameter backbone. The learning rate was 3 × 10⁻⁵ with 5%
warmup and cosine decay. Original-reference validation loss selected checkpoints; inference
retained English-first rewriting and the mT5 decoding settings above.

3.4 Reranking, wording refinement, and integration (V6)

V6 selected the highest-scoring Standard and Local replies among candidates proposed by
BM25, MiniLM retrieval, the V4 attention RNN, and the V5 transformer. While retrieval proposed
bank replies, the reranker evaluated these against generated options. This raised the question
of how accurately the reranker could pick the best response.

V6: From frozen MiniLM to task-specific training

Initial testing used a frozen MiniLM search reranker. Though it recognized related phrasing, it
struggled with speaker roles and answering the latest message, often confusing shared topics
with valid responses.

We fine-tuned MiniLM on 190 reviewed conversations (380 reference positives, 760 weak
negatives). On a 50-conversation development replay, appropriateness increased from 3.060 to
3.528 out of 5 for Standard and 3.796 to 3.878 for Local, outperforming a frozen style-aware
control (2.970 Standard, 3.810 Local).

Abbreviation augmentation (e.g., “tomorrow” → “tmr”) expanded training pairs to 2,556.
Standard appropriateness fell slightly while Local rose slightly, though increased
optimizer updates left the exact impact of augmentation inconclusive.

V7: Local Wording Refinement

V7 was created to inject local terms and boost style fit after we observed that the model
struggled with Singlish outputs, often producing standard English duplicates.

V7 v1 generated three lexicon variants from V6’s selected Local reply and used Qwen to select
the best option. Although Gemma rated its Local style fit at 4.875/5, Qwen occasionally selected
contextually awkward particles like "sia," suggesting Gemma might over-reward recognizable
Singlish terms. To prioritize context appropriateness, V7 v2 was adopted for the final demo to
apply subtler edits to SEA-LION’s Local reply.

V7 v2 applies dictionary substitutions (e.g., "tomorrow" → "tmr") to SEA-LION’s Local
reply, with each eligible phrase having a 50% replacement chance per attempt. Qwen
ranks the options if three distinct edits are available; otherwise, the original is returned.
The final demo uses SEA-LION directly for Standard replies and Qwen for Light Local
wording selection, while BM25, MiniLM, and V4 replies remain in the comparison view.

4. Evaluation and findings

4.1 Evaluation protocol

To evaluate candidate responses, we generated local and standard replies across 6 models
(BM25, MiniLM, RNN, SEALION, Reranker, V7) using identical test conversations. Due to time
and manpower constraints, we replaced blind human rating with an LLM evaluator, selecting
Gemma 12B after testing several options.

For each candidate reply, Gemma 12B received the context, incoming message, requested
style, and reply. It rated appropriateness, naturalness, and style fit (1–5 scale), and noted if a
simulated user would send it with minimal edits. To ensure diverse perspectives, we designed
10 student personas (aged 20–25) with varying texting styles and Singlish familiarity.

Across 60 conversations, 2 styles, 6 systems, and 10 personas, this setup yielded 7,200 rating
cells with consistent, correlated persona judgments.

4.2 System comparison and final demo choices

The original hypothesis predicted that combining models would improve reply quality.
The clearest exception was Standard English. Direct SEA-LION reached 4.555 for
appropriateness and 88.0% simulated would-send, while V6 cross-model reranking
reached 3.995 and 71.5%. The paired appropriateness difference for V6 was −0.560
points, with a 95% interval from −0.903 to −0.258. It shows that it is possible that
combining models can actually worsen the final result, especially when the reranker is
not optimized to select the best one based on the conversation context effectively. This
results in us foregoing the use of a reranker, instead directly taking from SEA-LION’s
outputs directly.

V7 follow up experiments

At our presentation, V7 v2’s Light Local replies scored 3.947/5 for style fit. To fix replies
sounding like standard English, we built V7 v3. V7 v2 returned SEA-LION’s original reply if
under three distinct variants were found. V7 v3 expanded the dictionary (e.g., adding “can ah”,
“can lor”), sampled edits across multiple phrases, and ranked up to six variants alongside SEA-
LION's output using a fixed Qwen reranker whenever a variant existed.

For example, for “we going to eat now, you want come mah?”, V7 v2 gave “Sure, let’s go!”,
whereas V7 v3 selected “Can lah, let’s go!”, boosting its style-fit score across ten Gemma
personas from 2.1 to 5.0. Overall, V7 v3 improved across all metrics, gaining most in style fit
over SEA-LION.

6. Limitations and future work

Our reviewed dataset is small for the variety of Singapore peer chats. Synthetic conversations
helped us explore more cases, but variations of the same template can still teach a model
narrow patterns. A higher priority is to curate more distinct situations, including rescheduling,
refusals, ambiguous references, and changes in speaker roles. Intent labels would help us
diagnose which kinds of replies improve.

Most quality scores came from one Gemma model with different fictional personas. The ratings
help compare candidates consistently, but the simulated would-send percentages cannot tell us
how often exchange students would actually send a suggestion. A follow-up could ask blinded
human reviewers to compare replies and allow both replies to be marked acceptable or
unsuitable. Those preferences could guide later reranker training.


7. Conclusion

Building ReplyCoach taught us that a good suggested reply has to fit the conversation before it
can sound local. The approach that worked best was to use SEA-LION to generate the reply,
then refine its wording for Light Local. Giving the reranker several ways to express the same
idea helped it find more natural local phrasing. Asking it to choose across different models was
harder, since a fluent reply could still miss a detail or misunderstand what the other person was
asking.

One of our biggest challenges was collecting enough Singaporean conversations with replies
that people would naturally send. Generating more examples helped us test more possibilities,
but it was still difficult to judge when a Singlish expression felt right. Using a local term could
raise the style score even when it sounded out of place in the conversation. In the end,
ReplyCoach gave users two ways to reply while leaving the choice to them. If we continued the
project, we would involve more Singaporean speakers in judging which replies they would
actually send and why.
